I was an early adopter of AI coding tools. In late 2021, still a student at Thapar, I jumped on GitHub Copilot the moment it hit the Student Developer Pack. I was instantly hooked on, I could churn out code faster, spin up multiple versions of the same feature, and even get nudged toward cleaner algorithms.However, my use of AI had been limited rather simple and small personal projects, where most of the coding guidelines were set by me or my friends, so mismatched coding styles didn’t bother us—as long as the code ran fine, we were satisfied.

### Joining Corporate
In early 2022 I joined JPMorgan Chase & Co. as a six-month software-development intern and began working on a huge Python codebase, where following the original author’s (who probably also an intern like me at the time) guidelines—now treated as the holy grail—was an absolute necessity if you wanted any change merged. AI had still not been introduced for everyday use at the bank. Since most of my time and energy went in understanding the office codebase, I had little motivation and energy to experiment with new things, and so came a long pause in my everyday use of AI coding tools. In hindsight, that took much of the fun out of coding.

### Days of Hard Labour
Since, AI was still not as popular in a corporate setting as it is today, it was on you to understand the codebase and modify it without breaking anyone else’s work. That meant diving deep, tracing dependencies, and double-checking every change—especially critical when real money is involved. I spent countless hours validating behaviour and making sure I understood every method I touched.

### And Then, The Glory
All that digging around in the codebase paid off. Within a few months I knew the code inside out. Adding features was easier because I already understood the functions involved; I was able to review colleagues’ code a lot more effectively and offer suggestions that helped. While, I still was not an expert, I could gauge the effort behind most tasks, and my teammates began relying on the tribal knowledge I’d built.

### ChatGPT Launches
By late 2022, ChatGPT went public and AI was suddenly everywhere. Every other day there was a new start-up doing something cool with AI. While, AI was still not something that was being actively used at corporate, it became something everyone would use off the clock, and well sometimes even while at work 😛. Soon almost every corporate had guidelines around the use of large language models at work. However, people soon realized that having guidelines around using AI at work was not the way to go about it, since people would still use AI no matter what the guidelines said, so corporates started providing their own approved tools, or partenering with companies who would offer said tools. Even so, my day-to-day coding still involved very little AI.

### *Approved tools become a thing*
Around mid-2024, JPMC started pushing its employees towards using approved AI tools, to *boost the productivity*. At first our project saw no AI-generated code because getting a licence was tough, but soon they became common and I got one. A fair chunk of my commits now were partly AI-generated. While the assistants which were made available to us did not have much information about the our codebase, so they would often give code which didn't work, but they still handeled boilerplate quite well reducing the overall effort. For new files, or new projects these tools were amazing, most of the time they would give functioning code, which required little tweaking so it fit in correctly with the whole framework. But those changes were quite easy for me to do as at this point I had a good idea about the code I was working on.

### I changed jobs
By the end of 2024 I wanted something new and joined HashiCorp’s Packer team. Riding the momentum from JPMC, I dived into the Packer codebase with AI as my sidekick. It felt effortless to make small changes—write a comment and bada-bing, bada-boom, Copilot produced the code. Yet after a month I didn’t feel nearly as confident as I had after my first month at JPMC. At first I blamed the codebase’s complexity, but soon I realised the real issue was my reliance on AI. Copilot was doing the heavy lifting and I was just tab-accepting, so I wasn’t building any knowledge or mental connections about the codebase.

### Turning off Copilot
So I disabled Copilot. I was still onboarding, so a temporary dip in productivity wouldn’t hurt anyone, as I wasn't expected to make any changes to `main`. Instantly after turning copilot off, I realized I would write a single line of code and wait a couple of seconds for Copilot to autocomplete it. Coding felt like a chore. After a few days, though, I hit my stride again. I still had to Google things—I was new to Go—but I enjoyed the process. Soon, I changed teams and started working on Vagrant, Copilot stayed off from day one and I built knowledge of the codebase much faster. Overall, I feel even though after turning off AI your productivity might decrease initally, and it might be hard to make changes, but once you truly understand the code, you can reintroduce AI tools to your workflow: you’re no longer blindly trusting the output but making informed decisions about which suggestions to keep.

### Onto the Last Part
If turning off AI is so great for learning, why is this website mostly AI-generated? Simple: I don’t enjoy front-end work. The first version of the site was a free template with content I copied over from my LinkedIn. When I wanted to add a blog, I thought AI could handle the boring and to some extent hard HTML/CSS work while I focused on the writing. AI excels at the mundane, and front-end chores feel mundane to me—so I let the AI do the heavy lifting.
